The eleven primitives

Every one is hand-rolled SVG against your tokens — no chart library, because a chart library is what would make this look like everyone else's tool. Each board shows the primitive at real size on Linesmith's own .lb-card, then the spec beneath it.

The data below is representative, not live — shapes and magnitudes chosen to be plausible for each table named in the spec strip. Nothing here was read from the database.

Your heat ramp: one finding, measured

I ran your ramp through a CVD validator rather than eyeballing it. The headline is better than I expected, and the one real problem is narrow and fixable.

Red-and-green is the canonical colourblind hazard, so I assumed lib/ui/heat.ts would fail. It doesn't. Your two poles clear the deuteranopia threshold: 

$ validate_palette.js "#c23b2c,#c98a1f,#0f7a4f" --mode light --pairs all

  [PASS] Lightness band      all 3 inside L 0.43–0.77
  [PASS] Chroma floor        all 3 >= 0.1
  [PASS] CVD separation      worst all-pairs #0f7a4f↔#c23b2c ΔE 8.4 (deutan)
  [PASS] Normal-vision floor worst all-pairs #c98a1f↔#c23b2c ΔE 17.9
  [WARN] Contrast vs surface below 3:1 — relief required: #c98a1f at 2.87:1

ΔE 8.4 against a floor of 8.0. It passes — by four tenths. That's a well-chosen pair (the green is dark and desaturated enough to separate from the red by lightness as well as hue), and it survives because of that, not by luck. But a margin that thin means secondary encoding isn't optional: sign, position, or a visible number has to carry the meaning too, wherever the ramp does real work.

The actual problem is the midpoint

heat.ts defines two ramps. COMPARE_STOPS goes red → neutral grey → green, and its comment reasons it out exactly right: a price in the middle of six books "is just ordinary," so the middle should be quiet. FILL_STOPS goes red → amber → green — and amber isn't quiet. Amber means caution.

So the fill ramp asserts "warning" everywhere the data only says "average." A 50% hit rate is a coin flip, not a caution. And amber is the one stop that fails contrast, at 2.87:1.

Today — FILL_STOPS

L 54.9 → 68.0 → 51.3 · arched

In greyscale the two poles collapse — 54.9 vs 51.3 is a 3.6-point difference — while the midpoint is the brightest thing on the scale. A reader scanning by value sees "average" as the extreme.

Proposed — sequential, for HeatGrid

L 92 → 45 · monotonic, one hue

Where the job is magnitude (the splits grid), one hue light→dark. Monotonic lightness means it survives greyscale, print and peripheral vision without depending on hue at all.

The distinction worth keeping

Your ramp isn't broken — it's a diverging ramp doing two jobs, and it's right for one of them. Keep red↔green (with the grey midpoint COMPARE_STOPS already uses) wherever polarity is the content: results against a line, contribution bars, price vs consensus. Use the monotonic single-hue ramp where the content is magnitude: the splits grid, density fills, anything scanned as a field.

One validator caveat, stated so nobody re-litigates it later: running the neutral-midpoint version through the script returns [FAIL] Chroma floor on the grey. That check is scoped to categorical palettes, where a grey slot would read as "no data." A diverging midpoint is supposed to be achromatic. The FAIL is the tool applying the wrong rule, not a real defect — worth writing down, because it will look like one the next time someone runs it.

A second thing I found while matching your tokens

To render these frames truthfully I used tailwind.config.ts verbatim. Doing that surfaced something: card is darker than paper.

paper
oklch(96%) — the page
card
oklch(93%) — three steps down
surface-subtle
oklch(96%) — back up again

Before the graphite reskin, page was #f7f5f0 and card was #FFFFFF — cards sat above the page, which is what a card is. The reskin held paper at 96% and dropped card to 93%, inverting it. Then surface-subtle, documented as an inset "inside cards," is 96% — lighter than the card it insets into.

Every frame on this page renders that faithfully, and honestly it doesn't look bad — it reads as recessed panels rather than raised cards, which is a legitimate look. But it's worth confirming it was chosen rather than inherited from a find-and-replace, because it's load-bearing for every surface in the app and it silently inverts the elevation model that shadow-card and .lb-card-hero assume.

Every primitive on one page, carrying an actual stat load — season line and ranks, the full Statcast block, ten splits × five windows, platoon splits, pitch mix, zone profile, twelve-game log, matchup, park and weather, market and model. This is what “see all the data in one place” has to mean before it means anything.

The reason it stays readable at this density is the grammar: one hover behaviour, one bar spec, one ramp, one type scale, one sample-size rule. Nothing here is a bespoke chart, so nothing here fights anything else. Depth is a layout problem once the marks agree.

What to build, in order

Phase 6 is unblocked as of today — the Phase 4 gate passed and CURRENT.md says so. This is the order I'd take it in, and the reasoning is the same one CLAUDE.md already records twice.

The argument, in one line

run_provider_specs exists because two of four hand-written job bodies forgot to check their rate limit. The sport adapters exist because three duplicated page components drifted until someone read them side by side. Ten bespoke charts will drift exactly the same way — a different hover here, a dashed grid there, a different empty state in each — and no single one of them will look wrong enough for anyone to file it.

  1. ChartFrame + useChartCrosshair first. Axes, grid, empty state, tooltip, hover sync — the parts every chart would otherwise re-invent. A day, maybe two, and it is what makes the rest cheap.
  2. Sparkline + SeriesChart. That is 6.1 — the highest-value item on the phase list, against 425,307 rows already sitting in Postgres with no consumer.
  3. Move DistributionChart into the library. No visual change. It stops being the one good chart and starts being the reference implementation.
  4. HeatGrid + PercentileRail + DensityCurve. The depth layer. All three read player_game_history, so they ship together and cost one data path between them.
  5. RangeBar, then ContributionBars. Dispersion first because it needs no model. Contribution last because it is the one that has to be right — it makes a claim about why.

One constraint worth naming now

PlayerDetail.tsx is 106KB and GameDetail.tsx is 105KB. A dozen inline charts makes both genuinely unworkable — the library isn't only about consistency, it's the only way the scope fits in files that are already at their limit.
